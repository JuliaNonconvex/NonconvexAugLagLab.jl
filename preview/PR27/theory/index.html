Theory: augmented Lagrangians, PBM and quasi-Newton sub-solvers

The augmented Lagrangian method

For the equality-constrained problem $\min f(x)$ s.t. $c(x) = 0$, the augmented Lagrangian (Hestenes; Powell; Rockafellar) is

\[L_\rho(x, y) = f(x) + y^\top c(x) + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\lVert c(x)\rVert^2.\]

The outer loop alternates:

  1. Primal step: $x^{k+1} \approx \arg\min_x L_{\rho_k}(x, y^k)$.
  2. Multiplier update: $y^{k+1} = y^k + \rho_k\, c(x^{k+1})$.

The update rule is forced by stationarity: the KKT system of the original problem pairs $\nabla f + J^\top y = 0$ with $c = 0$, and the subproblem stationarity $\nabla f(x^{k+1}) + J^\top (y^k + \rho_k c(x^{k+1})) = 0$ shows the parenthesized quantity is the next multiplier estimate. Rockafellar's interpretation: the ALM is the proximal point method on the dual function, which is why it converges under far weaker assumptions than the pure penalty method — the penalty $\rho$ no longer needs to tend to infinity, and a constant, large-enough $\rho$ suffices for local convergence. Inequality constraints enter through the max-form $g(x) \le 0 \leadsto \lambda^\top \max(g,0) + \frac{\rho}{2}\lVert\max(g,0)\rVert^2$ with the update $\lambda^{k+1} = \max(0, \lambda^k + \rho_k g(x^{k+1}))$.

The practical ALM of Birgin–Martínez

AugLagAlg follows the practical scheme of Birgin & Martínez (ALGENCAN):

  • Feasibility-driven penalty. The penalty is increased only when the feasibility did not improve enough. Two tolerance sequences drive the loop: $\eta_k$ (feasibility target — a step is "good" when $\lVert v(x)\rVert_\infty \le \eta_k$) and $\omega_k$ (how accurately the subproblem must be solved). After a good step both tighten with $1/\rho$; after a bad step the penalty grows geometrically and the tolerances reset.
  • Multiplier safeguards. The multipliers are updated only on good steps, avoiding the classical failure where noisy multipliers from poorly solved subproblems destabilize the next one.
  • Two-way adaptation (Curtis–Gould–Jiang–Robinson). A monotonically increasing penalty makes the subproblems ill-conditioned. When the multiplier update is small and feasibility improved, the penalty is allowed to decrease, keeping the subproblems tractable.
  • Infeasibility detection. If the penalty reaches its cap while the violation stagnates, the algorithm stops rather than looping forever.
  • Least-squares warm start (lsq_multipliers): the first multipliers solve $\min_\lambda \lVert \nabla f(x^0) + J(x^0)^\top \lambda \rVert^2$, a far better estimate than zero for equality-constrained problems.

The hardening stack (defaults in AugLagAlg)

On top of the Birgin–Martínez loop, AugLagAlg enables a set of hardening devices, each benchmark-gated on the TopOpt local-stress and Rosetta AC-OPF problems. They are the defaults because the measurements favored them; several (marked opt-in) are available but off by default because they trade one benchmark against the other.

Jacobian-free constraint scaling (scaling = true)

Before the ALM starts, each relaxed constraint is normalized by the magnitude of its gradient at the initial point, $\tilde c_i(x) = c_i(x)/\lVert\nabla c_i(x^0)\rVert$, and the objective by its initial magnitude. The penalty then acts on balanced, unit-scaled violations, so a single $\rho$ makes sense for all constraints — without the scaling, constraint groups with wildly different gradient scales need wildly different penalties, which is the classic source of ALM divergence on problems like AC-OPF (Curtis–Jiang–Robinson). The scaling uses only $Jv$ products (finite-difference), never a materialized Jacobian.

Matrix-free CG least-squares multiplier initialization (lsq_multipliers = true)

The first equality multipliers solve $\min_\lambda \lVert \nabla f(x^0) + J(x^0)^\top \lambda\rVert^2$ — the stationarity residual in multiplier space — by CGLS on the normal equations, again using only $Jv$ and $J^\top v$ products. The cost is a handful of constraint evaluations and the payoff is a multiplier estimate orders of magnitude better than zero, which removes the first many expensive outer iterations.

Safeguarded multipliers and selective penalty weights

The multiplier update is clamped ($|y|, \lambda \le 10^8$) so a poorly solved subproblem cannot poison the next one with a huge multiplier. Instead of escalating the global penalty when a single constraint group is violated, the selective update (weights) bumps only the weights of the most-violated groups (by ρ_incr, capped at ρ_max/ρ), escalating $\rho$ itself only when no single group stands out. This keeps the well-behaved groups' subproblems well-conditioned while the offender is penalized harder — the per-group generalization of the Birgin–Martínez feasibility-driven update.

Fletcher–Leyffer filter

The outer loop is globalized by a filter, not a merit function: a candidate step is rejected only if a previous iterate dominates it in both the AL merit and the constraint violation. This avoids the classic failure where a single scalar merit value misweights feasibility against optimality and rejects perfectly good steps (Fletcher–Leyffer 2002). The filter is reset when the penalty changes scale.

Anderson acceleration on the multiplier map (depth 2)

The multiplier update $y^{k+1} = y^k + \rho\, c(x^{k+1})$ is a fixed-point iteration, and near a solution its Jacobian is contractive (its spectral radius shrinks like $O(1/\rho)$). Anderson acceleration mixes the last $m$ fixed-point iterates, $y^{k+1} = \sum_j \theta_j (y^j + R^j)$ with the least-squares weights $\theta$ minimizing $\lVert\sum_j \theta_j R^j\rVert$, $R^j = (y^{j+1} - y^j)$ — an $m \times m$ solve per outer iteration that typically halves the outer count. The depth (2) and weight clamping ($\theta \in [-2, 2]$) are conservative defaults.

Penalty-scaled, warm-started L-BFGS (memory = 50)

The built-in sub-solver keeps one L-BFGS history across the whole outer loop (reset = false): the sequence of subproblems is a homotopy in $\rho$, so the secant pairs of the previous subproblem are excellent curvature for the next one. The two-loop base operator is $H_0 = I$ (the default, re-measured on 0.2.5: slightly better TopOpt objective and PGLib case5 feasibility than the diagonal scaling); the penalty-scaled diagonal $H_0 = (1+\rho)^{-1} I$ remains available as h0 = :penalty and keeps the first CG steps of each subproblem on the penalty's scale. The memory is a deliberate default of 50. Measured on the released 0.2.5 code: a small memory (5) is comparable but never better; a larger memory (100) leaves the TopOpt stress design essentially unchanged (+0.7% objective, +47% time) but materially improves equality-heavy AC-OPF — on PGLib case5 the gap drops 0.12 → 0.067 and the max scaled violation 1.14 → 0.70 at ~5× the wall-clock. Use memory = 100 when constraint feasibility dominates runtime.

Levenberg damping and stall retry

Inside the Steihaug–Toint solve the curvature is damped $B + \tau I$ with $\tau$ adapted by the trust-region ratio (Levenberg regularization): when steps are rejected, $\tau$ grows and the model becomes more conservative; when they succeed, $\tau$ shrinks back toward the secant curvature. A stall retry detects a subproblem that stopped progressing (the projected gradient no longer decreases) and restarts it with a relaxed tolerance rather than burning the outer budget on a dead solve.

TRON inner solver (linesearch = :tron, opt-in)

As an alternative to the Steihaug–Toint trust-region step, the subproblem $\min_{l \le x \le u,\; \lVert d\rVert \le \Delta} g^\top d + \frac12 d^\top B d$ can be solved by TRON (Lin & Moré 1999): projected conjugate gradients on the free-variable subspace with active-set identification — a variable that hits a bound mid-sweep is fixed for the rest of the sweep, negative curvature rides to the trust-region boundary, and the step respects both the bounds and the radius at every CG iterate. Measured on the released 0.2.5 code (after the L-BFGS matvec fix): on the TopOpt local-stress quick config it is 27% slower with a 1.8% worse objective; on PGLib case5 it improves the gap (0.12 → 0.095) and the max scaled violation (1.14 → 0.95) at comparable time, and on case14 it slightly improves feasibility (0.0144 → 0.013). It stays opt-in for problems where constraint feasibility dominates TopOpt-style speed.

Elastic mode (elastic = true, opt-in)

For equality-constrained problems whose subproblems risk infeasibility, the equalities are relaxed through slack variables, $y^\top(c - s) + \frac{\rho}{2}\lVert c - s\rVert^2 + \mu\sum_i\sqrt{s_i^2 + \epsilon^2}$, the elastic ALM: $s$ is solved along with $x$ in every subproblem, and the slack penalty $\mu$ absorbs constraint infeasibility. Off by default because the measured benchmarks do not need it.

Warm continuation (warm_continue = true, opt-in)

optimize! can be called repeatedly on the same Workspace, reusing the previous run's final iterate, multipliers (rescaled to the new constraint scales), per-group weights and the L-BFGS curvature object — exactly the state a continuation scheme needs. $\rho$, $\eta$, $\omega$, the filter and the Anderson history re-initialize from the new options, and a structure mismatch falls back to a cold start. Measured on the released 0.2.5 code (TopOpt local-stress SIMP continuation): warm continuation is consistently faster than cold restarts (26% on a coarse 3-step schedule, 10% on a fine 6-step one) and keeps the design at the stress threshold (violation 0.0 vs 0.005 on 3 steps, 0.003 vs 0.032 on 6 steps), while cold restarts end with a lighter objective but slightly violate the stress limit.

The penalty-barrier-multiplier method (PBM)

The classical ALM treats violations quadratically. The PBM of Ben-Tal & Zibulevsky (used by PENNON) replaces the inequality term with a shifted logarithmic barrier:

\[F(x, u, c) = f(x) + \sum_i c_i \log\!\left(1 + \frac{u_i g_i(x)}{c_i}\right), \qquad \frac{u_i g_i(x)}{c_i} > -1,\]

with the multiplier update $u_i^{k+1} = u_i^k \,/\, (1 + u_i^k g_i(x^k)/c_i)$ and a penalty increase when progress stalls. Stationarity of the subproblem gives $\nabla f + \sum_i \frac{u_i}{1 + u_i g_i / c_i} \nabla g_i = 0$, so the effective multiplier is $\lambda_i = u_i/(1 + u_i g_i/c_i)$ — the fixed point of the update is exactly the KKT complementarity, and on the feasible side ($g_i < 0$) the multipliers grow, sharpening the barrier near the boundary. In AugLagAlg the PBM term applies on the feasible side while the infeasible side falls back to the classical quadratic term — the standard globalization of the barrier domain (penalty = :pbm, or :mixed for quadratic semidefinite terms).

Projected ALM for semidefinite constraints

With a cone constraint $P(x) \succeq 0$ and a matrix multiplier $Y \succeq 0$, the projected augmented Lagrangian (Sun–Sun–Zhang) is

\[\varphi(x; Y, \rho) = \frac{1}{2\rho}\Big(\lVert \Pi_{\mathbb{S}_+^n}(Y - \rho P(x))\rVert_F^2 - \lVert Y\rVert_F^2\Big),\]

with the multiplier update $Y^{k+1} = \Pi_{\mathbb{S}_+^n}(Y^k - \rho_k P(x^{k+1}))$. The squared-distance-to-cone penalty is C¹ with gradient $-\langle \Pi_{\mathbb{S}_+^n}(Y - \rho P(x)), \nabla P(x)\rangle$, which NonconvexSemidefinite's project_psd rrule differentiates exactly. The scheme inherits the local (super)linear convergence rates of the classical ALM, and the subproblems are smooth NLPs solvable by any of the sub-solvers — including the built-in quasi-Newton driver.

Quasi-Newton sub-solvers

When every constraint is relaxed, each ALM subproblem is a smooth box-constrained NLP, solved by the built-in QuasiNewtonAlg. The theory:

  • Secant equation. A quasi-Newton model $B$ of the Hessian must satisfy $B_{k+1} s_k = y_k$ with $s_k = x^{k+1} - x^k$ and $y_k = \nabla f^{k+1} - \nabla f^k$.
  • BFGS. The minimal-norm update preserving symmetry and the secant equation is $B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{y y^\top}{y^\top s} - \frac{B_k s s^\top B_k}{s^\top B_k s}$, with the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury inverse update $H_{k+1} = (I - \rho s y^\top) H_k (I - \rho y s^\top) + \rho s s^\top$, $\rho = 1/(y^\top s)$ — the default :inverse form stores $H_k$, making the Newton step a single matrix–vector product; form = :hessian stores $B_k$ and factorizes.
  • Powell damping. If $y^\top s \le 0$ (nonconvex curvature), $y$ is blended toward $B_k s$ so the updated model stays positive definite.
  • L-BFGS. The two-loop recursion computes $B_k^{-1} g$ (and $B_k v$) from the last $m$ pairs in $O(mn)$ without storing a matrix; the base operator $H_0$ is the preconditioner (IdentityPreconditioner, DiagonalPreconditioner, or a user-defined operator).
  • Structured/sparse BFGS. The dense BFGS update destroys sparsity. The structure-preserving update of Fletcher restricts the dense update to the structural nonzeros of the container, so a sparse (or user-defined) Hessian keeps its pattern and its factorization works unchanged.
  • Trust region. The subproblem $\min_{\lVert d\rVert \le \Delta} g^\top d + \frac12 d^\top B d$ is solved with the Steihaug–Toint conjugate-gradient method, which terminates on the boundary or on negative curvature — robust for indefinite models. Paired with NewtonTRApprox (the exact Hessian through NonconvexCore's AD backends) this is the Newton trust-region method; the step is projected onto the box and accepted by the ratio of actual to predicted reduction.
  • Globalization. Line searches use Armijo backtracking along the projected direction; bounds are respected by projection, with the standard caveat that a projected step can stall on the boundary — the driver then shrinks the step or the radius.

The whole machinery is user-extensible through the AbstractHessianApprox interface: init!, apply!, update!, solve! and reset! — a custom matrix type with custom secant accumulation and a custom factorization plugs in directly.
